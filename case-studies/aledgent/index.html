The aledgent method

How one engineering lead, working with Claude and a team of AI agents, took a private, provable ledger from idea to a measured, multi-Agent design in under three weeks. The lead decided; the agents researched, wrote, built and measured.

The loop

Every piece of work ran through the same sequence. The order matters: nothing gets built before the lead has ruled on its shape.

  1. Idea. The lead states a goal in a sentence or two. "A canary this week." "Go straight to Kreivo." "3.5 ops/s is nothing."
  2. Investigate. Research agents read code, papers and data, and return sourced reports with file:line citations and run ids.
  3. Specify. A normative SPEC (requirements, invariants, open questions), an OVERVIEW, and a PLAN of features.
  4. Discuss. Meetings and chat produce rulings. Rulings are written down as data the same day.
  5. Decide by form. Every open question lands in a web form: grouped, concise, with a recommendation, defaults applied where safe. Answers flow back to the agents directly.
  6. Plan. Decisions become DECs, features, tasks and measurable gates.
  7. Execute. Agents work features in parallel, each in its own worktree, and merge through CI under fixed house rules.
  8. Measure. Claims are backed by runs on named hardware. Stale numbers get re-run.
  9. Amend. When reality disagrees with the spec, a numbered amendment changes it.
  10. Propose upward. Fixes that belong to dependencies become measured proposals for their maintainers.
  11. Report. Five-line chat updates; everything long goes into a page or the form, with a push notification.

Three episodes

1. Calling off the canary, then going further

Friday evening, the lead and DevOps called off the canary: the payment platform wasn't ready to connect. Instead of waiting, the lead asked three things at once: a sidecar so payment-rails can test against a real ledger in CI, a plan to wire it, and a direct path to the chain without an intermediate stage.

What the agents did

  • Published a sidecar image and a CI job on top of the existing client PR.
  • Mapped about 70 ledger touchpoints in payment-rails into 20 questions and 23 work packages.
  • Wrote the amendment, built the bind flow end to end and proved it on a test network the same weekend.

Why it worked

The pivot cost hours, not weeks, because the spec, the decisions and the plan were all data the agents could re-read.

2. "3.5 ops/s is basically nothing"

The first measurement of the core as a VOS Agent gave 3.5 operations per second. The lead refused to accept it as the ceiling and kept asking why.

Question the lead askedWhat measurement found
What's the overhead, and is it really the maintainer's to fix?The runtime re-hashes the actor's whole program on every call. We had a partial fix already, never applied together.
Is triple hashing a real security need?One verified pass is; the others repeat it. On our stack it was six passes, not three.
Is a JAM host unable to help?The Gray Paper has no hash host call by design, but its lookup hands over bytes already verified by hash.
Can we split the work across Agents?Yes: exactly-once credits between shards, proven by property tests, then measured on real hardware.

3. The decision form, designed by use

The way decisions were taken changed five times in two days, each time because the lead said what hurt.

  • Chat was too fast to read, so decisions moved to HTML pages with options and notes.
  • Pages lived on a laptop, so they became a published form with answers stored where the agents read them, plus push notifications.
  • 40 decisions in one list was a burden, so the form got sections and sub-tabs, with defaults already applied and marked.
  • Paragraphs were too long, so every item was rewritten to a title, a sentence and a few bullets.
  • Past answers were hard to find, so Decided became an Outbox, grouped like the inbox, showing each original question beside its answer.

Of 40 open decisions, 11 needed the lead; the rest only needed a glance.

Who did what

The lead

  • Set goals and scope, and called pivots.
  • Ruled on product and security trade-offs.
  • Challenged numbers and assumptions.
  • Talked to people: the VOS maintainer, DevOps.
  • Approved anything outward-facing: infrastructure, publishing, other teams' repos.

Claude and the agents

  • Research with citations; specs, amendments, plans.
  • Code, tests, CI and merges under house rules.
  • Benchmarks on named hardware, profiles, root causes.
  • Upstream proposals and bug reports, drafted for the lead to send.
  • The decision form and every update to it.

What made it work

  • Rulings are data. Every answer is stored and re-read by the next agent, never left in chat scrollback.
  • Defaults are explicit and reversible. Agents never block on a question: they take the conservative default, record it, and keep going.
  • Measure before claiming. Facts are tagged apart from inferences, and old numbers are re-run, not quoted.
  • House rules travel with every brief. Commit identity and signing, protected repositories, outward actions, concise reporting.
  • The lead's attention is the scarce resource. Five-line chat, grouped decisions, notifications only when something is ready.

What went wrong, and what changed

Commit identity overridden

An early agent brief forced an email address, leaving 224 commits unverified. Now a global rule forbids overriding identity or signing, and every brief carries it.

Too much text

Long chat answers went unread. Now chat is five lines at most, and everything else is a page or a form item.

Stale and invalid numbers

A batching bug silently voided some runs, and the stress verdict predated the speed-ups. Now runs are tied to commits, and gates get re-run.

Overclaims corrected by the lead

"Shared needs persistent disks", "8 ops/s is VOS's best", "the core runs on VOS": each was challenged and corrected with evidence.

What's next

The workflow is being turned into a product, SDD Studio: a decision inbox and outbox, living documentation linked both ways to decisions, and a plugin of workflow skills. This page is its first case study.
